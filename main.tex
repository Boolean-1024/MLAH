\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hypoglycemia, defined as blood glucose below 70 mg/dL, is a major concern in intensive care units (ICUs).\cite{ada2026hospital} Yeh et al.\cite{yeh2025hypoglycemia} reported that 12.7\% of ICU patients experienced hypoglycemia, which was associated with increased mortality. Similarly, Krinsley et al.\cite{krinsley2011mild} and Kalfon et al.\cite{kalfon2015severe} linked hypoglycemic episodes to higher mortality risk.

Despite initial recovery, patients may experience recurrent hypoglycemia, highlighting the need to identify those at continued risk.\cite{dnetto2016predictors}

This study uses MIMIC-IV to predict recurrent hypoglycemia within 24 hours of recovery. We compare baseline clinical features (Feature Set A) with additional glucose measurements and temporal features (Feature Set B), evaluating Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost to determine their predictive value.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data Source and Study Population}
\label{sec:data}

This retrospective study used MIMIC-IV version 3.1, containing de-identified electronic health records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center.\cite{johnson2023mimiciv,johnson2024mimiciv31}

Adults ($\geq$18 years) were identified using anchor age and year. ICU stays and demographics came from \textit{icustays} and \textit{patients}, respectively. Stays with missing or invalid timestamps were excluded.

Glucose measurements were extracted from \textit{chartevents} (220621, 226537) and \textit{labevents} (50809, 50931). Only numeric ICU measurements above 0 and at most 1,000 mg/dL were retained.

Measurements were merged chronologically and deduplicated by ICU stay and timestamp, prioritising laboratory values. Remaining same-source duplicates were resolved by retaining the minimum value; distinct timestamps were preserved.

Each ICU stay contributed at most one prediction instance, but patients could contribute multiple stays.

\subsection{Digital Phenotyping and Cohort Construction}
\label{sec:phenotype}

A rule-based digital phenotype identified hypoglycemia, recovery, and recurrence within 24 hours. The index event was the first glucose measurement below 70 mg/dL; recovery was the first subsequent measurement $\geq$70 mg/dL, marking the prediction time point.

Recurrence (label = 1) was glucose below 70 mg/dL within 24 hours after recovery. Non-recurrence (label = 0) required no observed recurrence, a full 24-hour ICU stay after recovery, and at least three post-recovery glucose measurements. Stays not meeting these criteria were excluded unless recurrence was observed.

Of 94,444 eligible adult ICU stays, 6,680 experienced hypoglycemia and 5,402 recovered. After excluding 1,326 stays with insufficient follow-up and 2,170 with insufficient glucose measurements, the cohort included 1,906 stays from 1,823 patients, with 627 (32.9\%) positive and 1,279 (67.1\%) negative cases.

Checks confirmed unique stay identifiers, valid chronology, and label consistency. However, differing follow-up requirements may introduce monitoring-related selection bias.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

All predictors used only information recorded at or before recovery, and glucose values after recovery were used only to assign the label. Glucose features followed the same extraction rules as the phenotype.

We built 76 candidate features (Table~\ref{tab:features}), using derived concepts from the MIMIC Code Repository where available.\cite{johnson2018code} SOFA was the last hourly score before recovery. Sepsis-3\cite{singer2016sepsis3} was counted only when the SOFA rise, the antibiotic and the culture all preceded recovery, and insulin infusions were prorated to the 24 hours before the index event. A missing medication or nutrition record was coded as 0. The seven comorbidity variables are the exception to the timing rule: they come from ICD codes assigned at discharge, so we treated them as a retrospective approximation and repeated the full analysis without them (Table~\ref{tab:s4}).

Feature Set A (56 features) contained the baseline clinical information, including the index glucose value. Feature Set B added 20 features describing the glucose trajectory before recovery. Missingness was compared between outcome groups with chi-square tests, and no feature exceeded the prespecified 50\% exclusion threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}).

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

For logistic regression (LR) and random forest (RF), missing values of continuous variables were imputed with the training-fold median, and a flag was added to record whether each value was missing. Missing values of binary variables were filled with the most frequent training value, keeping the 0/1 encoding. In all models, categorical variables were one-hot encoded, with missing values labelled ``Missing''.

For LR, we applied an inverse hyperbolic sine (arcsinh) transformation to skewed continuous variables and standardized all continuous variables. For RF, continuous variables were not transformed or standardized. For XGBoost, continuous and binary features were used without imputation or scaling: trees split on cut-off values, and XGBoost learns which branch a missing value should follow, so the absence of a test can itself carry information, as for lactate (Section~\ref{sec:cohort}). All imputation values, standardization parameters and encoded categories were learned on the training folds and applied to the corresponding validation folds to avoid data leakage.


\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\subsubsection{Logistic Regression and Random Forest}

We established prediction models using LR and RF, and trained each model using both feature sets A and B, resulting in four models: LR-A, LR-B, RF-A, and RF-B.

LR is an interpretable baseline model. The model coefficients indicate how the transformed features are associated with the log odds of recurrence, holding the other features constant. Due to the imbalance in the number of recurrent and non-recurrent samples, we set \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} and used regularization to control the complexity of the model. We compared the two regularization methods, L1 and L2, and tested four C values, namely 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10. 

RF can capture the non-linear relationships and interactions between features that LR finds difficult to handle by combining the prediction results of multiple decision trees.\cite{breiman2001random} We fixed the number of decision trees at 200, and the number of features considered for each split was set to the square root of the number of features after preprocessing. Additionally, we compared different maximum depths, the minimum number of samples per leaf node, and class weights. 

\subsubsection{XGBoost}

XGBoost builds shallow decision trees in sequence, each correcting the errors of the previous ones,\cite{chen2016xgboost} whereas RF builds its trees independently and averages them. Tree ensembles often perform well on medium-sized tabular datasets.\cite{grinsztajn2022tree} We trained XGB-A and XGB-B, with each tree using a random 80\% of stays and 80\% of features. We compared tree depths (3 or 5), learning rates (0.03 or 0.1), numbers of trees (200 or 500), minimum child weights (1 or 5) and the positive-class weight (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}: 1, or 2.08, the ratio of non-recurrent to recurrent stays in the training set).

To describe what XGBoost relied on, we used SHAP values,\cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local} which show how much each feature pushed a prediction towards or away from recurrence. Overall importance was the mean absolute SHAP value, and dependence plots showed how each effect changed across feature values. These analyses describe model behavior, not causal effects, and were not used for feature selection or retuning.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning and Validation}
\label{sec:tuning}

All six models LR-A, LR-B, RF-A, RF-B, XGB-A, and XGB-B used the same patient-level split, with 20\% of patients held out for testing. Nested cross-validation used five outer and three inner patient-grouped folds, with hyperparameters selected by mean validation average precision (AP). To obtain out-of-fold (OOF) predictions, we held out one validation fold in each outer iteration and only used the remaining patients’ data for inner cross-validation and parameter selection.\cite{cawley2010overfitting} After selecting the hyperparameters, we refitted the model on the outer training set and made predictions on the held-out validation fold. Therefore, the OOF prediction probability for each sample is derived from a model that did not use any data from that patient's records during training or hyperparameter selection. These OOF predictions were used for threshold selection.

After obtaining all the OOF predictions, we used the full training set to re-select the hyperparameters through the fixed five-fold cross-validation, and then re-train each final model on the full training set. The independent test set was not used for model training or hyperparameter selection. We then used the six final models to generate recurrence probabilities for the test set. For XGBoost, early stopping was not used. Search grids and selected values for XGBoost are listed in Table~\ref{tab:s3}.

\subsection{Model Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluated six fixed models on 383 test ICU stays from 365 patients to predict documented recurrence.

Sensitivity was the primary operating metric because the intended use was to identify patients for closer glucose monitoring after recovery. For each model, we selected the highest threshold that achieved at least 80\% sensitivity on the training nested out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. This minimized alerts while meeting the target. The 80\% target was an exploratory choice, not a clinical standard. Thresholds were fixed before the test predictions were evaluated.

We reported AUROC, average precision (AP), sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and alert rate. Percentile 95\% confidence intervals used 2,000 patient-level bootstrap samples, retaining all stays per sampled patient. Common samples were used for paired B-minus-A comparisons, with models and thresholds fixed. We also examined OOF targets of 70\% and 90\%. Subsequent error analysis grouped test stays by index glucose below 54 versus 54 to below 70 mg/dL.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Cohort Characteristics}
\label{sec:cohort}

Median age was 65 years (IQR 54 to 75) and 56.7\% of stays were male (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). Age, sex and SOFA score were similar between outcome groups. Recurrent stays had higher lactate (median 3.4 vs 1.9 mmol/L), lower mean glucose in the 24 hours before recovery (83.6 vs 99.8 mg/dL) and longer index episodes (5.8 vs 5.0 hours), and their nadir fell below 54 mg/dL more often (36.4\% vs 26.7\%). Fewer of them had been on an insulin infusion before the index event (11.3\% vs 27.1\%). Lactate, glucose trajectory and insulin exposure features showed the largest standardized mean differences (Figure~\ref{fig:smd}).\cite{austin2009standardized} When trajectories were aligned on recovery, recurrent stays already had lower glucose before the index event and stayed near 80 to 90 mg/dL afterwards, while non-recurrent stays returned to about 120 mg/dL (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}).

Missingness differed by outcome.\cite{agniel2018biases} Lactate was missing in 20.3\% of recurrent stays and 10.6\% of non-recurrent stays. Pre-index glucose summaries were missing in 27.1\% and 17.5\%, because recurrent stays more often had hypoglycemia at their first ICU glucose measurement. The first recurrence came a median of 8.9 hours after recovery (IQR 4.0 to 14.2), and 39\% of recurrent stays had fewer than three measurements in the outcome window (Figure~\ref{fig:s3}), which follows from the different follow-up rules for the two groups (Section~\ref{sec:phenotype}).

\subsection{Model Performance}
\label{sec:performance}

The test set contained 133 stays with documented recurrence and 250 labelled non-recurrent. Table~\ref{tab:performance} summarizes performance. LR-B had the highest AUROC (0.697) and AP (0.533) estimates; XGB-B had the highest F1-score (0.571). Figure~\ref{fig:rocpr} compares discrimination across thresholds, not just the selected operating points.

RF-A identified 116 of 133 recurrent stays but generated 178 false positives and flagged 76.8\% of test stays. RF-A, RF-B and XGB-B reached 80\% test sensitivity. The other models achieved 66.2\%--76.7\%, despite meeting the target in their OOF predictions.

\subsection{Feature Set Comparison and Robustness}
\label{sec:comparison}

Feature set B increased AUROC and AP estimates for all algorithms. The paired AUROC gains were 0.043 for LR (95\% CI 0.005--0.080), 0.050 for RF (0.007--0.092), and 0.044 for XGBoost (0.001--0.086). AP gains were 0.015, 0.038, and 0.058, respectively; all three AP intervals included zero (Figure~\ref{fig:gain}).

Higher ranking performance did not always produce higher sensitivity at the chosen thresholds. Compared with RF-A, RF-B reduced false positives from 178 to 139 but missed 8 more recurrent stays. Increasing the OOF sensitivity target raised test sensitivity and alert rates across all models. For LR-B, the 70\% and 90\% targets produced test sensitivities of 59.4\% and 86.5\%, with alert rates of 43.1\% and 72.3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:s1}).

In XGB-B, lactate and recovery glucose ranked highest, and 8 of the top 15 features were glucose-trajectory features (Figure~\ref{fig:s4}). Their SHAP patterns were non-linear, and missing lactate increased predicted risk (Figure~\ref{fig:s5}).

Removing the seven discharge-coded comorbidity variables changed test AUROC by at most 0.012 and AP by at most 0.014 for any model, and Feature Set B still had a higher AUROC than A for every algorithm (gains 0.038 to 0.041; Table~\ref{tab:s4}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Across all three algorithms, the glucose trajectory before recovery improved prediction. This was the most consistent of our findings, with AUROC gains of 0.043 to 0.050 over baseline clinical features. Patients who recurred had often recovered only partially. A single reading above the hypoglycemia threshold is therefore weak evidence that the episode is over. Lactate was also a strong predictor. This points to illness severity as a driver of recurrence, though it is an association, not a causal finding.

Accuracy still limits clinical use. To reach high sensitivity, the models flagged 52.7\% to 76.8\% of test stays. Most of these alerts would go to patients who do not recur. The models could prompt extra glucose checks, but they are not ready to drive interruptive alerts.

These results come with several limitations, and the most important lies in how the cohort was built. A single low reading was enough to label a stay as recurrent. A non-recurrent label required a full day in the ICU and repeated glucose measurements. The two groups therefore entered the cohort under different rules, and 39\% of recurrent stays would not have met the measurement requirement. The models predict recurrence as it was observed under these recording rules, which is not the same as the true risk for every patient. Feature Set B also includes measures of how often glucose was checked. Its gain likely reflects monitoring behavior as well as physiology, although the strongest trajectory features were the glucose values themselves. In ICUs that check glucose more or less often, both the features and the chance of observing a recurrence would differ, so performance may not transfer.

Future work will focus on external validation and a time-to-event outcome. The latter would keep stays with incomplete follow-up.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Added glucose-related features increased AUROC and AP estimates across all three algorithms, although AP gains remained uncertain. These findings support further study of glucose information for predicting documented recurrence after recovery. Clinical use requires verification of predictor availability, consistent outcome monitoring, and validation in other settings.
